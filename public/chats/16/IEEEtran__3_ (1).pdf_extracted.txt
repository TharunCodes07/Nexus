theAbstract—This research presents a method for estimating state of charge (SOC) of lithium-ion batteries using a neural network model. The primary challenges of this study include accurately capturing the nonlinear behavior of battery SOC under various driving conditions and temperatures. The approach involved initially cleaning the dataset and calculating the SOC values. The data is then scaled appropriately, and the neural network model is trained using diverse driving cycles and a combination of these cycles at various temperatures. The trained model was evaluated on both a single driving cycle and a mixed driving cycle, which integrated all driving conditions. The results demonstrate that the model achieves an R2 score of over 0.98 in SOC estimation, indicating its robustness and reliability across different scenarios. This high level of accuracy suggests that the neural network model worked well in real-life battery management systems, making accurate SOC predictions in a wide range of operating conditions.

Index Terms—SOC(State Of Charge), Lithium-ion Batteries, Neural Network, Battery Management Systems, Driving Cycles

# I. INTRODUCTION

Lithium-ion batteries play a vital role in electric vehicles (EVs) and aim to perform well in short to medium-range distances. The key idea behind the usage of lithium-ion (Li-ion) batteries is their high energy density, which in turn leads to a high battery life cycle. The usage of Li-ion cells in EVs indeed reduces pollution. Given the extensive use of Li-ion cells, there is now a need to optimize battery usage, which follows the Battery Management System (BMS). The BMS acts as a key in ensuring the safe, reliable, and efficient management of battery-powered applications. The key factor that promotes long battery life is preventing overcharging and deep discharging, which, if left unmonitored, may lead to reduced performance. This paper focuses on state of charge (SOC) estimation, a technique the Battery Management System (BMS) uses to optimize battery usage and improve performance. The SOC is mainly the ratio of the capacity of the battery that is remaining by the total capacity of the battery, which is a relative quantity. There are quite a few ways for the estimation of SOC, including coulomb counting, open circuit voltage, and model-based approaches. However, these methods often don’t account for behavioral changes due to factors like temperature variations, battery aging, and complex use patterns. Battery usage and discharging patterns may vary as per the usage pattern; on a highway, the vehicle travels at an almost constant speed, resulting in a more consistent energy demand from the battery. At the same time, if the vehicle is traveling on a city road where the speed keeps varying due to factors like bad roads or traffic, this leads to rapid battery discharge.

# II. METHODOLOGY

# A. SOC Calculation – Coulomb Counting Approach

Coulomb counting is one of the most popular techniques used to calculate SOC. This method calculates the charge that is being transferred to the battery while charging and the charge that gets transferred from the battery to the load; it is mathematically expressed as,

SOC(t) = SOC(t0) + 1 ∫t0t (I(t) dt) / CN

where:

- SOC(t0) is the initial state of charge at time t0.
- CN is the nominal capacity of the battery.

The term ∫t0t (I(t) dt) / Capacityn represents the change in charge over time. This integral calculates the net charge that has flowed into or out of the battery from t0 to t. Let ∆Q = ∫t0t I(t) dt, which is the net charge change. Then:

SOC(t) = Qinit + ∆Q / Capacityn

The current capacity of the battery at time t, Capacityr, is the sum of the initial change and the net change over the time t0 to t:

Capacityr = Qinit + ∆Q

The SOC as a fraction of the nominal capacity is:

SOC(t) = Capacityn / Capacityr

To express the SOC as a percentage, multiply by 100:

SOC% = (Capacityr / Capacityn) × 100

Under controlled conditions, Coulomb counting can provide SOC estimates with an accuracy of ±1-5%, meaning the estimated SOC can be within 1-5% above or below the actual SOC.

# B. Proposed Methodology for SOC Prediction in Lithium-Ion Batteries

This study introduces an innovative approach for accurately predicting the SOC of lithium-ion batteries across diverse temperature conditions. Accurate SOC estimation is crucial for ensuring optimal performance and longevity of batteries in various applications. Traditional methods face significant challenges in capturing batteries’ nonlinear and dynamic behavior, particularly under varying temperatures, which is a crucial factor in practical applications.

To address these challenges, a method that integrates LSTM neural networks with a Simple Moving Average (SMA) filter for noise reduction has been used. LSTM networks are capable of learning and remembering long-term dependencies within sequential data, thus making them a prime candidate for capturing patterns of battery behavior [13]. Additionally, the SMA filter is employed to smooth out the predictions, mitigating the impact of noise and enhancing the stability of the results.

# C. Methodological Framework

Neural networks are mathematical models designed to mimic the brain’s structure and function. They consist of interconnected layers of neurons that process input data to generate outputs. Connections between neurons have weights that are adjusted during training to minimize model loss.

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) are a class of neural networks specifically designed to handle sequential data. Unlike classical feed-forward neural networks, RNNs perform better in scenarios where the order of data points matters by incorporating temporal dynamics, as they allow information to persist. However, RNNs come with certain drawbacks, such as exploding and vanishing gradients, which hinder their performance over long sequences by either amplifying or diminishing the gradients excessively.

In RNNs, the hidden state ht at time step t is calculated as follows:

ht = tanh(Wh · ht−1 + Wx · xt + bh)

where:

- tanh is the activation function.
- Wh and Wx are the weights for the hidden state and input, respectively.
- ht−1 is the previous hidden state.
- xt is the current input.
- bh is the bias term.

To address these issues, LSTM networks introduce a unique architecture that includes memory cells and a gating mechanism that helps control the flow of information. Their ability to retain and process information over long sequences without the gradient issues encountered by RNNs makes them highly effective for sequential tasks [14,1].

They achieve this through a memory cell with three gates, the forget gate ft, the input gate it, and the output gate ot.

ft is responsible for controlling the extent to which the previous cell state Ct−1 is remembered:

ft = σ(Wf · [ht−1, x] + bf)

where:

- σ is the sigmoid function.
- Wf stands for the weights.
- ht−1 is the previous hidden state.
- xt is the current input.
- bf is the bias term.

it is responsible for controlling the extent to which the new information has to be stored in the cell state:

it = σ(Wi · [ht−1, xt] + bi)

C˜t = tanh(WC · [ht−1, xt] + bC)

Ct = ft · Ct−1 + it · C˜t

The output gate ot controls the information to be outputted based on the cell state:

ot = σ(Wo · [ht−1, x] + bo)

ht = ot · tanh(Ct)

where ht represents the current hidden state output. This gated architecture of the LSTM model allows it to selectively remember and forget information over extended sequences, making it a powerful tool when working with sequential data.

An SMA filter is employed to mitigate the impact of noise inherent in sensor measurements. SMA is calculated using the equation:

SMAt = (xt−1 + xt−2 + ... + xt−N) / N

where:

- SMAt is the Simple Moving Average at time t,
- xt−1, xt−2, ..., xt−N are the data points over the past N periods,
- N is the number of periods used to calculate the SMA.

This filtering mechanism smooths out short-term fluctuations in SOC predictions, thereby enhancing the stability and accuracy of the final estimations.

# E. Training and Testing of the model

When training a model, it learns to map independent features to predict the dependent variable. The model’s success largely depends on the quality and relevance of the provided features. Effective training requires ample data that guides the prediction of the dependent variable.

When it comes to SOC prediction, features such as Voltage, Current and Temperature can be used as input features. When a battery has a higher capacity, the voltage is at its highest and reduces as the capacity decreases. One other important factor which influences SOC values is the temperature at which the battery is functioning.

To accommodate all these factors into training a dataset which was made from observations across six different temperatures (-20 °C, -10 °C, 0 °C, 10 °C, 25 °C and 40 °C) on different kinds of driving cycles having different driving patterns (US06, UDDS, LA92, HWFET & and two mixed files from each temp which is a combination of all these driving cycles) with an overall size of about 23,62,098 rows thus leading to different discharge rate has been provided to the model for it to learn. While the SOC was calculated it was ensured that the battery aging was taken care of. As the battery was continuously used for testing its total capacity value dropped thus affecting the final total SOC value.

Before providing the model with this data, the data has been preprocessed into X (independent features) and Y (dependent feature) NumPy arrays. The data is scaled into the range of [0,1] to give the model an insight into the range in which it has to predict which helps the model converge faster and also makes sure that all features are taken into consideration equally and no feature dominates over the other because of its high magnitude. The MinMax Scalar has been used for this purpose.

Each driving cycle is scaled separately and then added to the feature vectors. This is because each cycle consists of values from 100% - 0% and so scaling them this way will preserve the intricate patterns of the dataset and the finally processed data was fit into the model with an ADAM optimization function along with the LogCosh loss function.

The Log-Cosh loss function is defined as:

L(y, ˆ) = yi log (cosh (yi − ˆ))

where:

• y is the true value,

• yˆ is the predicted value,

• cosh(x) is the hyperbolic cosine function defined as cosh(x) = (ex + e−x)/2.

On training, it is noticed that the model’s predictions are extremely noisy and irrelevant when a low sequence length is used, once the sequence length is increased over 100 the model’s loss goes down rapidly, the reason behind this is that the values in the dataset have only a short change of SOC value across each instance. Due to this, the model is unable to learn any patterns out of it due to the very less change in SOC values. In the end after a lot of experimentation, 150 is finalized as the ideal sequence length.

To ensure the model doesn’t overfit the training dataset, dropouts and callbacks like early stopping and learning rate schedulers are used. A patience value of 3 is used for the learning rate scheduler with a reduction factor of 0.5 and a patience value of 6 is used for the early stopping. Once the model is trained and applied to the testing dataset, the noise in the predicted values is reduced using an SMA filter with a window size of 150. These hyper-parameters were determined experimentally.

Compared to traditional SOC estimation methods, like the Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) and Particle Filter (PF), the neural network model offers greater accuracy and resilience to nonlinearities, particularly across temperature variations. Lower RMSE and MAE values and a consistent R² close to 98% demonstrate its superior ability to capture complex battery behavior under real-world conditions.

# IV. CONCLUSION

In this paper, the application of an LSTM model combined with SMA for SOC estimation in BMS. By leveraging the sequential nature of battery data, the LSTM-based model has demonstrated significant improvements. It achieved an R2 score of over 0.98, which is a substantial improvement compared to traditional models. The LSTM model generalizes well over a wide range of temperatures. It maintains consistent prediction accuracy, making it more reliable in practical applications. The integration of SMA enhances the reliability of the predictions. It smooths out short-term fluctuations and noise. The consistent high accuracy across various scenarios highlights the robustness and effectiveness of the LSTM model in real-world applications. This innovative approach opens new possibilities for advanced battery management solutions. It could further fine-tune the process and improve the model’s ability to identify different driving cycles and their patterns, especially when combined. This contributes to more reliable battery systems in the future.
